A paper from my MSc dissertation

How much of public sentiment is really the public, and how much is just the platform?

Lots of studies show sentiment differs across Twitter, Reddit, YouTube and Quora, then stop. My contribution is to treat each platform as a measuring instrument, so I can separate how much of that difference is the platform itself from how much is real disagreement, and put the platforms on one comparable scale. As far as I can find, no one has done this for machine-labelled sentiment.

Why none of these is novel enough to carry a paper on its own
Each one applies an established method to a new dataset. Solid work, and the integrated four-platform UK dataset has real value, but none is a research-level novelty on its own:
  • Multi-platform sentiment has been done. Pellert et al. 2022 validated text sentiment across platforms against self-reports, and the Measuring Hate Speech corpus (Kennedy et al. 2020, preprint; peer-reviewed as Sachdeva et al. 2022) spans YouTube, Twitter and Reddit. The new part here is the UK economic-policy domain, not the method.
  • Transformers beating older models has been settled since Devlin et al. 2019, and confirmed for policy sentiment elsewhere (for example Souza et al. 2024). Showing it again on a new dataset confirms a known result rather than extending it.
  • Forecasting opinion from social media goes back to O'Connor et al. 2010, who tied tweet sentiment to consumer-confidence series (more reliably than to candidate-preference polls); Prophet (Taylor & Letham 2018) is just the tool. Short-term predictability of sentiment is not itself a new claim.
That is exactly why the section below exists. The dataset and domain are valuable, but the publishable novelty is the measurement reframing, not the pipeline, the classifier, or the forecast.

What is not yet there: a rigorous same-event, platform-by-platform comparison that isolates measurement differences from topic composition and who-posts effects. That is the gap the paper fills.

What we add before publishing

My contribution the novelty

Plenty of studies show sentiment differs across platforms, then stop there. I treat each platform as a measuring instrument, the way a survey researcher treats phone, online and face to face as three ways of asking the same question. That one shift turns a loose observation into something you can measure, test, and correct.

My version (new)

Social platform

Twitter, Reddit, YouTube, Quora. The same opinion can surface differently on each one.

On top of the dissertation above, four concrete moves turn it into a publishable paper. As far as I can find, they have not been brought together for machine-labelled sentiment across platforms before:

01the reframe

Platform as measuring instrument

Treat each platform as the instrument doing the measuring, the way a survey treats phone, web and face to face. This turns "platforms differ" from a finding you report into a measurement problem you can test.

02main result

Variance split: platform vs. real disagreement

A generalizability-theory decomposition that puts a number on how much of the cross-platform gap is the platform itself and how much is genuine disagreement, using NRC emotion components as indicators and a re-checked human sample as the independent yardstick.

03reusable

Invariance and DIF testing

Measurement invariance and differential item functioning, run on several emotion signals per event, to say which platform comparisons are sound before you trust them.

04first step

Test equating onto one scale

Use shared policy events as anchor items to line the platforms onto one comparable scale. Where no stable anchor exists, the honest answer is that they cannot be fairly compared, which is itself worth reporting.

How this is unique

Each piece exists on its own. What I cannot find anywhere is the combination: measurement-equivalence tools, applied to machine-labelled sentiment, compared across two or more platforms, with the platform itself as the thing being tested. The nearest work each stops one step short, survey self-report rather than model-labelled text (Boyd et al. 2024), annotators rather than platforms (Sachdeva et al. 2022), or correlation rather than equating (Pellert et al. 2022). An adversarial literature sweep in August 2026 confirmed the combination is still unoccupied, and added two fresh neighbours that each stop one step short in a new way: Pokropek 2026 brings full CFA invariance testing to text measures, but on one platform with time, not platform, as the grouping; and generalizability theory has now reached LLM-annotation facets (prompt, model, extraction), still not the platform facet. One sharpening worth stating plainly: the Kennedy et al. corpus does span Reddit, YouTube and Twitter, but its modelled facets are items and annotators; platform remains an unmodelled sampling stratum.

To be clear about what is mine: the split between how it is measured and who posts is not mine to claim. Survey work drew it first (Sen et al., Schober et al.). The closest adjacent use of the toolkit is Cernat et al. 2025, who run a multitrait-multimethod model treating survey and digital-trace data as different methods, though not across social platforms and not on sentiment. What is new is bringing the full toolkit onto the platform itself, for machine-labelled sentiment across platforms. I am not claiming to recover a "true" opinion, only a comparable one.

What the contribution delivers
New knowledge
It turns a question that is normally assumed away into a measured quantity: how much of a cross-platform sentiment gap is real opinion, and how much is a recording artefact of the platform.
In practice

My dissertation measured taxation at about 65% negative, with Twitter spiking sharper than YouTube on the same announcements. Is that a real split in opinion, or just Twitter's format amplifying anger? This puts a number on it, where Pellert et al. (2022) validated cross-platform sentiment against self-reports but never separated the two.

How others build on it
A drop-in diagnostic. Any team comparing sentiment across platforms can run the invariance check to see whether their comparison holds, and equate the scales where it does. It turns a one-off finding into a reusable instrument.
In practice

A researcher comparing welfare-reform sentiment across Reddit and YouTube could run the same check before pooling them into one trend, rather than assuming the scales match. That is the reusable measurement step Schober et al. (2016) argued social media analysis needs.

What it advances
It gives the "social media as a barometer of opinion" claim a falsifiable measurement footing, advancing computational social science, public-opinion measurement, and the validity toolkit for applied NLP.
In practice

Before a cross-platform sentiment index is reported as public opinion, this says which platforms are safe to combine and which are not. It is the kind of total-error scrutiny Sen et al. (2021) brought to digital traces, now carried to machine-labelled sentiment.

Where this could lead

The same diagnostic, in the hands of the organisations that already watch social sentiment, and what it lets them build.

Design safeguards, added after an adversarial methods review
Each of these preempts a specific, citable objection a quantitative reviewer would raise:
  • Platform is a fixed facet. Two to four named platforms are neither random nor exchangeable, so the G-study treats platform as fixed (posts, events and coders as random), and every conclusion is scoped to the studied platforms rather than "platforms in general".
  • Categorical indicators, effect-size criteria. NRC emotion components are sparse count indicators, so invariance is tested in categorical-indicator models (threshold, not intercept, invariance), judged by effect sizes rather than chi-square p-values, which are uninterpretable at social-media sample sizes.
  • The lexicon-artifact control. Apparent platform non-invariance could be dictionary coverage (slang, length norms) rather than a mode effect (Ludwig et al. 2022). Every equivalence test is therefore replicated with a second, non-lexicon measure (the fine-tuned transformer); only convergent non-invariance is interpreted as a mode effect, and disagreement is reported as instrument artifact.
  • Anchors are purified, and linking is the honest word. Anchor items with their own DIF contaminate everything downstream, so anchors are purified ex ante, an anchor-free DIF estimator (Chen et al. 2023) runs as robustness, step 5 is described as linking rather than full equating, and a leave-one-event-out sensitivity check guards the fiscal-event anchors.
  • Invariance is diagnostic, not a licence. The methods literature is currently debating whether passing invariance is either necessary or sufficient for valid comparison (Robitzsch & Lüdtke 2023). Results here feed the naive-versus-adjusted simulation as evidence, rather than acting as a binary gate, and alignment estimation (Muthén & Asparouhov 2018) runs alongside the exact sequence.
  • The gold set is probability-sampled. The 1,000 to 1,500 human labels are drawn with known sampling probabilities, stratified by platform and event, so design-based correction of the multilevel estimates (Egami et al. 2023) comes free with the yardstick.
  • Nesting is respected. Posts sit inside events, so invariance is examined in a two-level framework (Jak et al. 2013), separating within-event platform non-equivalence from between-event composition differences.
The survey-mode literature already runs mode-as-group DIF on web versus paper questionnaires; this design carries that precedent to platforms as the modes and machine labels as the indicators.
Why the platform part is the hard part

Two kinds of bias, not one

There are two reasons social media can mislead you. The first is well studied. The second, the one I work on, is mostly ignored for sentiment pulled from text, and you cannot fix it the same way.

Already studied

Who posts

The wrong people post, or the slice you collect is skewed, so the crowd is not representative. You fix it by re-weighting to match the population.

What I work on

How it is measured

Observations suggest that even when the opinion is the same, each platform records it differently. Re-weighting cannot fix that, and neither can the new label-error corrections: methods now exist to fix classifier error in downstream estimates (Egami et al. 2023) and to quantify how model choice flips conclusions (Baumann et al. 2025), but they treat the label, not the mode. The method below is designed to test whether platform non-equivalence is real, then measure and ideally correct it.

Why this fits ICWSM

Where it sits in the conference

ICWSM has a long line of work asking whether social media data faithfully measures public opinion. This paper is a direct continuation of it, moving the question from "is the sample representative" to "is the measurement equivalent."

Tufekci (2014) set out the validity and representativeness problems of social media big data at ICWSM, including the field's over-reliance on a single platform. Morstatter et al. (2013) asked whether the data you pull even represents the platform it comes from. Cohen and Ruths (2013) showed that attitude inference from social media had been systematically over-reported because validation samples were skewed. O'Connor et al. (2010) linked text sentiment to opinion series, but treated platform noise as something that averages out.

These are about who is in the data, how it is sampled, and whether inferred labels can be trusted. The same line runs straight through the newest proceedings. Greene et al. (2025) show the same politicians read ideologically different on Twitter and Facebook and warn against single-platform designs. Atreja et al. (2025) show LLM labels shift with model and prompt choice, so the labelling instrument itself is unstable. Nogara et al. (2025) catch a single classifier scoring identical content as more toxic in German than in English: instrument non-equivalence across languages, documented. And cross-platform machine-labelled comparisons are being published right now with equivalence assumed rather than tested, from affective polarization on Facebook and Twitter (Overgaard et al. 2024) to sentiment on Douyin versus TikTok (Wei et al. 2026), while post-API data access makes multi-platform corpora ever more heterogeneous (Goanta et al. 2026; Davidson et al. 2023).

None of it asks whether the same opinion is recorded equivalently across platforms once a model has labelled it. What Nogara and colleagues show for language, this paper tests for platform, with formal psychometric tools rather than ad hoc comparison. That is the fit: the same long-standing ICWSM concern, carried to the next unexamined source of error.

Honest limits

What this does not do

I would rather be upfront about the edges. These are the things a careful reviewer will ask about.
  • Sentiment is not the same as support. I measure the mood in the text, which stands in for opinion. I keep that distinction clear.
  • It is observational, and this is the biggest caveat. The same event draws different people and different sub-topics on each platform, so what I put down to the platform is an upper bound until I can follow the same users across platforms. I keep that front and centre.
  • It is these four platforms, or fewer. The findings are about them, not every platform, and if the anchor-density audit shows some platforms too sparse, the comparison narrows to the dense ones rather than stretching thin data.
  • The counts are being recomputed. The dissertation per-platform figures are internally inconsistent (they sum to roughly 395k against the 279k headline), so every count in the paper will be recomputed from the raw data before analysis. Better that we report this than a reviewer finds it.
  • Known errata in the linked dissertation. A final self-audit found five internal inconsistencies a careful reader may notice: Figure 7.5 is mislabelled "Facebook" (Facebook was excluded); the Prophet MAE appears as ~0.02 in Chapter 7 but ~0.05 in Table 8.1; the post-calibration ECE appears as 0.03 in Chapter 7 but 0.05 in Chapter 6; the manual-labelling coverage appears as 0.07% in Section 5.5 but ~0.5% in Section 7.4; and the conclusion rounds accuracy to ~0.89 against Table 7.1's 88.06%. The dissertation is the awarded record and stands as submitted; the paper recomputes and supersedes every affected figure.
  • The fix needs a real test. Lining the platforms up is step one. Checking whether mode-adjusted sentiment tracks a UK opinion series (British Social Attitudes, British Election Study, or YouGov economic trackers) would come later, but the contribution does not depend on it.

"To my knowledge, I am the first to treat each platform as a measurement mode and apply the psychometric equivalence-and-equating toolkit to machine-classified policy sentiment, with the platform itself as the grouping facet, testing whether platform differences reflect genuine attitudinal divergence or mode-induced measurement non-equivalence, and reporting where cross-platform comparison is and is not justified."

Part 2 · The review, answered
Independent review, August 2026

What the reviewer said, and what we did about it

Dr Haig asked Eni, a colleague with ICWSM experience, to pressure-test this brief. She ran a five-part critique (assisted by her institution's enterprise AI tools) against the brief and the dissertation itself. Every substantive point is listed below with its answer. Nothing is hidden: two points changed the plan materially, several were accepted outright, and two get a reasoned pushback.

The reviewer's overall verdict: the conceptual framing is rock solid and the contribution is a textbook definition of a meaningful academic contribution. The problems are all about the data, not the idea. The revised plan below is the answer to exactly that. Update, 21 August: an adversarial literature sweep (an arXiv API scan of every paper mentioning the core psychometric terms, plus the ICWSM 2024-2026 proceedings and the usual indexes) re-tested the novelty claim against 2023-2026 work. It survived; the nearest new neighbours and the resulting hardening are recorded in the change log.

1. The validation set is too small

accepted, and extended
200 hand-labelled posts cannot anchor a generalizability or DIF analysis over 279,000; expand to roughly 1,000 to 1,500, evenly split across platforms.
Accepted in full: the gold set grows to 1,000 to 1,500 posts drawn from the anchor-event windows, split evenly, with a second annotator on a subset so agreement can be defended properly. We go one step further than the review: the classifier was originally trained on lexicon-seeded (VADER) labels, so the old 200-post sample was doing double duty. The expanded human sample is what makes the yardstick genuinely independent, which a measurement paper cannot do without.

2. No pre-identified anchor events

accepted
Invariance and equating need synchronised external shocks across all platforms; without pre-identifying them you cannot know whether the corpus has enough overlapping data. A fiscal announcement might mean 20,000 tweets but five Reddit threads and zero YouTube videos (the zero-cell problem).
Accepted. The plan now begins with an anchor-density audit: 5 to 10 named UK fiscal shocks (the September 2022 mini-budget as centrepiece, plus Budgets, Autumn Statements and Bank of England decisions) mapped onto the corpus, with post counts per platform in 48-hour and two-week windows. The audit decides everything downstream before any analysis is trusted.

3. Is a policy announcement a stable anchor at all?

answered, open for discussion
On Twitter an announcement triggers immediate reactions; on Reddit the debate may arrive three days later. Can such events serve as invariant anchor items across fundamentally different communication rhythms?
The design answer: use both tight (48-hour) and wide (two-week) windows, include time-since-event in the multilevel model, and treat platform reaction latency as part of the mode effect being measured rather than as noise to remove. If latency profiles differ systematically, that is itself evidence about how platforms record the same public moment. This is the main methodological question flagged for the next supervision meeting.

4. The dataset is too small for a modern ICWSM empirical paper

reframed, with the reviewer
279k posts across four platforms averages roughly 70k per platform; modern ICWSM empirical papers often expect millions, and spread over four topics and several years the daily volume is sparse.
Partly a pushback the reviewer herself endorsed: the millions-of-posts bar applies to empirical-discovery papers. This is now explicitly a measurement-methods paper, where density inside the anchor windows matters more than raw corpus size. If the audit still shows thin cells, the fallback is a targeted patch of new collection in exactly those windows, which is squarely within my professional capability.

5. The per-platform counts do not add up

accepted, found independently
The reviewer's own platform figures (Twitter ~120k, Reddit ~109k, YouTube ~25k, Quora ~25k) do not match the dissertation tables.
Confirmed and worse than the review noticed: the dissertation text itself gives Reddit ~130k and YouTube ~120k, which sums to roughly 395k against the 279k headline. Neither set of figures can be trusted as written. Recounting every total from the raw data is now step zero, and the limits section of the proposal says so publicly.

6. Units of measurement differ across platforms

partly the point
A tweet, a Reddit thread, a Quora essay and a YouTube comment are structurally different units; they cannot be treated as identical parallel test items.
Partly a flaw, partly the premise: that platforms record the same opinion in structurally different ways is exactly what the paper measures. Operationally it is handled by fixing the unit of analysis: a sentiment score per post, aggregated to event-by-platform cells, with the measurement machinery working at the cell level rather than pretending posts are parallel items.

7. No conversational-depth metric

acknowledged, deferred
Each comment was treated as a flat text string; messages-per-post and conversational structure were never captured.
True, and acknowledged as a limitation. It does not affect the invariance machinery, which operates on per-event sentiment scores, but conversational depth is noted as future work and would matter for any follow-up on how discussion structure shapes expressed sentiment.

8. The selection confound

already conceded, kept front and centre
Without following the same users across platforms, how cleanly can "how it is measured" be separated from "who posts"?
It cannot be fully separated, and the proposal says so: the platform effect is an upper bound until matched users exist. The multilevel model, holding event and topic mix constant, narrows the gap observationally; the honest label stays on the result.

9. Should this be a poster or short paper rather than a full paper?

accepted, and now verified
Given the data constraints, the poster track, which explicitly welcomes novel frameworks still proving out their data, may be the right first venue.
Accepted, and the mechanics are now verified against the official ICWSM 2027 call: the 15 September 2026 round accepts full papers only, while posters (up to 5 pages) are accepted in the 15 January 2027 round. The plan is therefore: reframed analysis and annotation first, arXiv preprint by December 2026, poster to the January round, full paper to follow.
