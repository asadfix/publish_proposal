\section{Introduction}
Cross-platform comparisons of machine-labelled text are now routine: affective polarization compared across Facebook and Twitter \citep{overgaard2024}, sentiment compared across Douyin and TikTok \citep{wei2026}, with platform differences interpreted substantively. The instrument itself, meanwhile, is known to be unstable: the same classifier scores identical content as more toxic in German than in English \citep{nogara2025}, and LLM labels shift with prompt and model choice \citep{atreja2025}. What no study has asked is whether the same opinion is recorded equivalently across platforms once a model has labelled it: whether the platform is part of the measurement.

Survey methodology answered the analogous question decades ago: the same respondent answers differently by phone, on paper and online, and mode effects are measured, tested and corrected \citep{klausch2013,hox2015}.
